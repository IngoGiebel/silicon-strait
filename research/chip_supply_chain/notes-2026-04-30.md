## Why this thread, and why now

Recognition diplomacy (Day 2) handled the *interpretive* layer of the cross-strait conflict. Chip supply chain handles the *material* layer — the physical asset whose location and concentration give the conflict its asymmetric stakes. Without TSMC, the scenarios in Day 4+ (military balance, integration scenarios, US strategic options) collapse into a different problem class. The chip-fab geography is what converts a regional sovereignty dispute into a global-economy single-point-of-failure. **The Silicon Shield doctrine is the bridge concept** — it's the working hypothesis that links materiel concentration to deterrence outcomes, and the GWW3 model needs to either ratify it, refute it, or expose its boundary conditions.

## Five anchor facts (with sources)

### Fact 1 — TSMC's foundry-market dominance and advanced-node monopoly
TSMC holds **~70% of global foundry-market revenue** [Source 1] and **>90% of the most-advanced-generation chip production** [Source 2]. The "advanced generation" (5nm and below) is where the AI-accelerator and high-end smartphone supply chains terminate. No other foundry can substitute at advanced nodes today; Samsung Foundry holds second place at 5nm/3nm but with different IP libraries and worse yield, and Intel Foundry Services is years behind in customer adoption.

### Fact 2 — Geographic concentration: Taiwan-resident capacity
TSMC operates fabs at Hsinchu (Fabs 2, 3, 5, 8, 12A, 12B, 20), Taichung (Fab 15), Tainan (Fab 18), Shanhua (Fabs 6, 14), and Kaohsiung (Fab 22) — i.e. virtually the entire fab footprint is on the western corridor of Taiwan within ~250km of the strait [Source 1]. 2025 production: 15.02 million 12-inch equivalent wafers [Source 1]. Diversification efforts:
- **Arizona Fab 21:** Phase 1 operational; phases 2 production targeted 2028; phases 3-5 targeted mass production 2030 [Source 1]
- **Kumamoto Japan Fab 23:** Commercial operations began December 2024; second factory under construction adjacent as of January 2025 [Source 1]
- **Dresden Germany:** Planned operational 2029, monthly capacity 40,000 12-inch wafers [Source 1]

The diversification timeline is the load-bearing point: even fully built out, the overseas footprint is **trailing-node** (Arizona phase 1 = N4/N3, not the leading edge), and the leading-edge node (currently N2 entering production at Hsinchu Fab 22, soon Fab 18 P8) **stays on Taiwan through at least 2030**. The Silicon Shield doctrine bets on this concentration. Diversification efforts dilute it on the trailing edge first.

### Fact 3 — ASML: the upstream chokepoint
ASML holds **~83% of worldwide lithography-machine sales** and a **near-monopoly in EUV** [Source 3]. The High-NA platform (numerical aperture 0.55, ~$370M per system) shipped first to Intel (December 2023) and TSMC (late 2024) — those are the only confirmed High-NA recipients [Source 3]. EUV is the necessary enabling tooling for sub-7nm at production-scale economics; without it, advanced-node production requires multi-patterning DUV with severe cost and yield penalties (see Fact 5).

### Fact 4 — Export-control regime timeline (Netherlands ↔ US ↔ China)
The Dutch export-control regime against Chinese EUV/DUV access tightened across five steps [Source 3]:
- 2018: First Trump-era US pressure on ASML to block China sales
- March 2023: Dutch government places restrictions on chip equipment exports
- September 2023: Export-license requirements come into effect
- January 2024: Restrictions extended to additional advanced equipment
- September 6, 2024: Tighter controls on certain ASML chipmaking equipment
- January 2025: Restrictions expanded; ASML now applies to Dutch government directly (rather than via US channel) — a regime-formalization step

### Fact 5 — SMIC's DUV multi-patterning workaround and its ceiling
SMIC operates the **N+3 process** as of December 2025, confirmed via TechInsights teardown of Huawei Kirin 9030 (November 2025 product) [Source 4]. Process is "scaled evolution of 7nm-class" using **DUV immersion lithography with self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP)** — explicitly *not* EUV [Source 4]. SMIC has been on the US Entity List since December 2020 [Source 4]. Practical implications:
- 7nm officially established July 2022
- 5nm-equivalent capability deployed by late 2024 for AI/smartphone (Kirin 9000s September 2023, Kirin 9030 November 2025)
- Wikipedia does not provide yield rates; industry reports (uncited here, to verify on Day 3) suggest ~50% yield vs TSMC's >90% at equivalent nodes — drives 2-3× higher per-die cost

The DUV-multi-patterning route works for boutique-volume strategic products (Huawei flagship phones, datacenter AI accelerators in tens of thousands of units), but it does not scale to civilian economic dependence: pricing dynamics break.

## Preliminary GWW3 framing seeds (to develop in Day 3 publication)

### Framing 1 — Asymmetric incomplete information on the substitution timeline
PRC and US/Taiwan players in the chip game face **different information sets** on when SMIC closes the gap. PRC information set treats N+3 deployment as proof-of-concept that EUV can be substituted; US/Taiwan information set treats it as boutique-only with structural cost ceiling. **Both can be coherent under their own priors**; neither is currently testing the other's hypothesis under common-knowledge conditions. This is a **signaling-game** structure, not a complete-information bargaining game. Belief-subgraph will need to model PRC-state-belief vs PRC-citizenry-belief vs PRC-firm-belief separately — they diverge sharply on substitution feasibility.

### Framing 2 — The Silicon Shield as a Pottinger-deterrence assumption-set
The Silicon Shield doctrine [Source 2] requires three coupled assumptions:
- **A1 (mutual vulnerability):** disruption hurts all major parties simultaneously
- **A2 (rational-actor):** all major parties weight expected economic cost in their conflict-decision calculus
- **A3 (irreversibility):** chip-supply disruption from a kinetic conflict cannot be quickly mitigated by overseas capacity

Pottinger's 2022 critique [Source 2] specifically rejects A2 for PRC decision-making. GWW3 needs to **make this rejection-or-acceptance an explicit player parameter**, not bury it in assumed rationality. If A2 is even partially relaxed for the PRC player, the deterrent effect of the Silicon Shield drops nonlinearly — and the model should expose that nonlinearity.

### Framing 3 — Decoupling timeline as a Schelling clock
If A1 erodes (overseas capacity reaches threshold X% of global advanced-node by year Y), the Silicon Shield assumption-set fails by construction. The CHIPS Act ($50B subsidies, Biden 2022 — see [Source 2]) and the Trump-administration pivot to American manufacturing [Source 2] are both *accelerators of A1-erosion*. The model should produce a **time-dependent deterrence parameter** that decays with the global-share-of-Taiwan-fabs metric. Calibration target: per public TSMC roadmap, leading-edge stays on Taiwan through 2030; the deterrence parameter has a hard floor until then.

### Framing 4 — Markov vs path-dependent, again
Day 2's recognition-diplomacy thesis ("Markov property fails") has a chip-supply analogue: **substitution capacity is path-dependent on tooling, talent, and IP — not just on capex**. SMIC's N+3 timing depends on when ASML last shipped DUV scanners pre-September-2023 controls and on SMIC's hoarding of spares; that's a path-dependence on regulatory chronology, not a stationary parameter. Same modeling discipline as Day 2: don't model substitution as a memoryless process even when the textbook formulation is tempting.
