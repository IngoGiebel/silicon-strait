## Research findings

### S1. From strategic ambiguity to strategic translucency — the legislative trace

Strategic ambiguity, as conventionally narrated, is a 1995–2024 doctrine: the United States deliberately refuses to commit to defending Taiwan, in order to deter both PRC adventurism *and* Taiwanese unilateral declarations of independence. The doctrine's surface — Lake's 1995 Senate testimony, Bush-43's 2001 "whatever it took" reversal-and-walkback, Biden's four 2021–2022 verbal commitments-and-walkbacks, Trump-2's 2026 transactional Q1 signaling — is the layer most foreign-policy commentary observes, debates, and projects into the future.

**The substrate beneath that surface is the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979 and its 2020–2024 legislative thickening.** The TRA's verbatim text, locked from `law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/22/3301` and `/3302` [1][2], discloses three structural features that the doctrinal-ambiguity framing systematically obscures:

**First, the TRA is policy-not-commitment language throughout.** §3301(b) is six declarative *policy* statements ("It is the policy of the United States—"); none uses the language of treaty obligation or defense commitment. The verbatim phrase that comes closest to a commitment, §3301(b)(4)'s "grave concern" framing of any non-peaceful effort to determine Taiwan's future, is deliberately weaker than NATO Art. 5's "armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all" — a comparison the 1979 drafters knew they were inviting and declined.

**Second, twin discretionary triggers.** §3302(b) places quantity-of-arms determination in the joint hands of President and Congress; §3302(c) places threat-response determination in the same joint hands. Neither clause specifies *what* response is required; both specify only the *procedure* by which the response is determined. This is structural translucency: the law does not promise a particular act, only a particular institutional process.

**Third, two separate arms-mandate hooks.** §3301(b)(5) (provide Taiwan with defensive arms — *substantive* mandate) and §3302(a) (make available such defense articles as may be necessary — *procedural* mandate) together form the legislative double-anchor that survives any single executive's reluctance. Trump-2 can slow-walk §3302(a) notifications but cannot repeal §3301(b)(5) without congressional action.

The 1982 document-pair — the Six Assurances [3] and the August 17 Communiqué [4] — is older than the 1995-onward "ambiguity" doctrine and stronger evidence of structural translucency than any post-1995 textual trace. The 1982 documents are, by deliberate design, **mutually contradictory**: the August 17 Communiqué states that the United States "intends gradually to reduce its sale of arms to Taiwan, leading, over a period of time, to a final resolution," while the Six Assurances simultaneously deny any end-date for arms sales and any prior consultation with the PRC over arms sales [3]. This is not an accidental tension; it is the structural origin of strategic translucency, the textual layer in which the post-1995 ambiguity doctrine is anchored.

The 2020–2024 legislative cadence is the **densification phase**:

- **TAIPEI Act of 2019** (P.L. 116-135, enacted 2020-03-26) [5] — directs the Secretary of State to consider reducing US engagement with countries that take "actions against Taiwan" and to advocate for Taiwan's membership/observership in international organizations not requiring statehood. Bipartisan unanimous Senate, voice-vote House — a legislative-density signal, not a partisan one.

- **Taiwan Enhanced Resilience Act** (FY2023 NDAA Subtitle, P.L. 117-263, 2022-12-23) [6] — five-year, $10 billion Foreign Military Financing authorization for Taiwan; first-time direct grants (not loans). This is the single largest legislative shift in US-Taiwan defense relations since the TRA itself. It survives Trump-2's transactional pressure because the FMF authorization is appropriated through 2027.

- **FY2024 NDAA** (P.L. 118-31) [7] — expanded TERA training authorities; Taiwan-specific war-reserve stockpile authority.

- **FY2025 NDAA** (P.L. 118-159) [8] — Pacific Deterrence Initiative funded at $11.5 billion; Taiwan-related items roughly $300 million tagged.

Each NDAA cycle adds a Taiwan-specific authority that survives an executive-branch policy pivot. The cumulative drag of 2020–2024 NDAA additions means even a deliberately reluctant 2026 administration delivers more substantive Taiwan support than the 2017–2020 baseline.

**Adm. Samuel J. Paparo's SASC posture statement (2026-04-22)** [9] is the executive-branch operational translation of this legislative substrate. Paparo's load-bearing line — *"We can't want Taiwan's defense more than they want it itself"* — is read by surface commentary as a softening of the US commitment. Read against the TRA verbatim, it is the opposite: it is a precise restatement of TRA §3302(a)'s "self-defense capability" language. The 1979 phrase already encoded *"Taiwan defends Taiwan, US enables capacity"*; Paparo's 2026 testimony reads as an explicit *operationalization* of that 1979 substrate, not a departure from it.

Paparo's three meta-trends in the operating environment, verbatim from his prepared statement [9]:

> "Information, influence, cognitive and cyber operations are achieving increasing strategic effects by shaping perceptions and disrupting decision-making."

> "The commoditization of cheap, massed, uncrewed and often autonomous systems has lowered barriers to advanced capabilities, increasing the cost of assault operations and compressing decision timelines."

> "The commoditization of long-range, precision, penetrating and — frequently — cheap strike has enabled greater leverage to coercion and cost imposition."

Each of the three meta-trends maps onto a sub-thread of this report: trend 1 (perception-shaping / decision-disruption) feeds the GWW3 game-theoretic frame (§S8); trend 2 (cost-of-assault / decision-timeline compression) is the operational variable that the Davidson Window (§S2) must now account for; trend 3 (long-range precision strike) is the cost-imposition mechanism that Day-4's military-balance publication established as the structural shape of 2026 cross-strait deterrence.

The continuity is itself the thesis: translucency persists across administrations *because* the legislative substrate persists. Paparo speaks under Trump-2 in 2026 in language structurally identical to the 1979 statute drafted under Carter — the substrate-not-rhetoric test, and it passes.

### S2. The Davidson Window — and what survives in 2026

Adm. Philip Davidson's March-2021 SASC posture statement [10] is the canonical text for what is now called "the Davidson Window" — the framing that PRC military capability for a forced reunification against Taiwan would be plausibly available "within the next six years," i.e. by 2027. The verbatim line, surfaced by contemporaneous coverage rather than the prepared-statement PDF body, reads:

> "Taiwan is clearly one of their ambitions before then. And I think the threat is manifest during this decade, in fact in the next six years."

Two things matter for 2026 analysis. **First, the prepared-statement text — not the headline quote — is the more durable contribution.** Davidson's prepared SASC statement [10] anchors three structural commitments that survived into the Aquilino, then Paparo, INDOPACOM postures:

> "Any recommendations for additional studies, including a requirement for only mobile systems, disregard the immediacy and complexity of the threat (2026) and the absolute need to integrate fires across the region from a forward multi-domain command and control node west of the International Date Line (IDL)."

The **2026 immediacy framing** — written in 2021 — is the textual basis for the Pacific Deterrence Initiative budget structure that the FY2024 and FY2025 NDAAs (§S1) appropriate against. Davidson's prepared statement is the single source most directly responsible for the present shape of the PDI line item:

> "Funding levels in PDI for FY21 equates to seven-tenths of 1% of the DOD's Total Obligating Authority in FY21, and just two-thirds the amount spent on the European Defense Initiative in FY20 ($5.9B)." [10]

By 2026, PDI is appropriated at $11.5 billion — a roughly four-fold real increase, traceable in the public NDAA conference reports — and the legislative justification language in those reports cites Davidson's prepared statement directly. Davidson's PDI argument is therefore not merely historic; it is the citation-anchor for $11.5 billion of FY26 force-posture spend.

**Second, the First-Island-Chain doctrine.** Davidson's prepared statement frames the operational concept that subsequent commanders have continued to execute:

> "USINDOPACOM must field an integrated Joint Force with precision-strike networks along the First and Second Island Chains; an integrated air and missile defense in the Second Island Chain; and an enhanced force posture that provides the ability to preserve regional stability, and if needed, disperse and sustain combat operations for extended periods." [10]

Read against Paparo's three meta-trends (§S1), this is the operational template into which Paparo's 2026 testimony slots. Paparo's commoditized-precision-strike trend is the inverse of Davidson's "precision-strike networks along the First and Second Island Chains" — both sides are now arming the same geometry, which is exactly the cost-imposition equilibrium that Day-4's military-balance publication identified.

**Third, the conventional-deterrence erosion claim.** Davidson, 2021:

> "The greatest danger for the United States is the erosion of conventional deterrence. Without a valid and convincing conventional deterrent, the PRC will be emboldened to take action in the region to supplant U.S. interests. As the military balance in the Indo-Pacific becomes more unfavorable to the United States, we are accumulating additional risk that may embolden our adversaries and competitors to attempt unilaterally changing the status quo." [10]

This is the 2021 source-text for the 2024–2026 capability-gap discourse that Day-4 of this report series unpacked in detail. The 2026 operative question is whether the gap has been narrowed (PDI argument), held flat (status-quo argument), or widened (PRC-modernization-outpacing-US-rebuild argument). S3 (arms-sales pipeline) and S4 (alliance multiplication) will produce the empirical input for that question.

### S3. Arms-sales pipeline — backlog as pseudo-alliance

The arms-sales pipeline is the operationalization of TRA §3301(b)(5)'s mandate that the United States make available to Taiwan "such defense articles and defense services in such quantity as may be necessary to enable Taiwan to maintain a sufficient self-defense capability" [Source 1] — and as of 2026 it is, by the rigorous public-tracking measurement, a **$32 billion structural commitment**.

The Taiwan Security Monitor (Schar School of Policy and Government, George Mason University; principal authors Eric Gomez and Joseph O'Connor) maintains the most rigorous public-tracking dataset for the backlog, anchored in the Arms Export Control Act's congressional-notification requirement: "The Arms Export Control Act requires that Congress receive notification of new FMS cases above a certain dollar threshold before those cases can advance. After notification, Congress has a specified period, depending on the recipient country (30 days in Taiwan's case), during which it can block the sale by passing a joint resolution of disapproval." [Source 11] As of 31 January 2026, "the arms sale backlog as of January 31, 2026, remains $32.0 billion." [Source 12]

The composition of the backlog is the load-bearing structural fact, not the topline. As of December 2025, asymmetric capabilities account for "$14.7 billion out of $32 billion (46%)" [Source 11], up from "$4.2 billion out of $19.17 billion, or 22 percent" when the dataset launched in late 2023 [Source 11]. Across that 27-month window, the absolute backlog roughly doubled (~$19.2 B → $32.0 B; growth factor ~1.67) while the asymmetric component more than tripled (~$4.2 B → $14.7 B; growth factor ~3.5). Asymmetric-system absorption is therefore outpacing traditional-system absorption by a factor of more than two — a structural shift that ratifies, in pipeline-substrate form, the asymmetric-defense reorientation Day-4 documented at the doctrinal level (the "Overall Defense Concept" lineage and the post-Han Kuang reform path).

The largest unfulfilled FMS cases as of December 2025 are HIMARS launchers and munitions ($4.05 billion), M109A7 Paladin self-propelled howitzers ($4.03 billion), ALTIUS loitering munitions/drones ($1.1 billion), and the Tactical Mission Network software/equipment package ($1.01 billion) [Source 11]. The longest-pending case is illustrative of the pipeline's character: Stinger missiles were "originally notified in December 2015 and July 2019, finally delivered by end of 2025—approximately 10 years for initial notification to completion." [Source 11] Pipeline tempo is therefore not a near-term binding constraint on PLA planners; the binding constraint at the strategic-modeling layer is the *cumulative density* of the pipeline, of which any specific delivery date is a probabilistic component.

Trump-2 era trajectory matters because the pipeline must continue to absorb new notifications to remain a deterrent. The December 2025 announcement covered approximately $11 billion (the largest single Taiwan FMS package on record) and a further package of up to $20 billion is reported as pending but not yet formally notified to Congress; the cumulative Trump-1 record (2017–2021) was approximately $18.3 billion across four years, against Obama's two-term ~$20 billion and Biden's four-year ~$8.4 billion [Source 13]. The single-package size in December 2025 alone is ~60% of Trump-1's full four-year cumulative — which empirically refutes the early-2025 "Trump will starve the pipeline" reading and ratifies the strategic-translucency thesis from S1: pipeline velocity is governed by the legislatively-encoded commitment substrate (TERA's $10 billion FMF authorization at [Source 6]; NDAA-cycle Taiwan provisions at [Sources 6, 7, 8]) more than by executive transactional preference. The Day-7 thesis on translucency hardens: the *probability distribution* over US response-types in a Taiwan crisis is shaped less by the day-of-crisis executive than by the depth and inertia of the legislatively-encoded commitment substrate. The $32 billion FMS backlog is the largest single empirical instance of that substrate.

The strategic upshot is therefore counterintuitive: **the backlog itself is the deterrent.** Every weapons system in queue is a system the PRC must plan against in any forced-reunification scenario regardless of its specific delivery date, because the commitment-to-deliver is congressionally encoded and cannot be unilaterally rescinded by the executive branch. The pipeline converts a logistical phenomenon (delivery delay, typically read as a US weakness) into a strategic phenomenon (depth of declared commitment, structurally read as a US strength). And it is precisely *that* conversion that makes the arms-sales pipeline the closest functional equivalent the US-Taiwan relationship has to a treaty alliance — without the structural fragility of a formal one (which would be vulnerable to a single inter-branch repudiation event), and without the credibility paradox of an explicit defense guarantee (which would, under translucent-commitment theory, *reduce* deterrence by removing the calibrated ambiguity of the response-distribution).

### S4. Alliance multiplication — the Camp David trilateral as load-bearing scaffold

The structurally most consequential 2023–2026 development for the US-Taiwan strategic options menu is not located in the US-Taiwan dyad at all. It is located in the trilateral US-Japan-ROK framework that was inaugurated at Camp David on 18 August 2023 and has since been institutionalized in a way that survives administration transitions. The Camp David trilateral is the second encoding mechanism — alongside the legislative substrate documented in S1 — by which US commitment to Taiwan Strait stability is encoded *structurally* rather than declaratively. Where S1 encoded that commitment in domestic statute, S4 encodes it in alliance-lattice geometry.

The 2023 founding documents — the *Spirit of Camp David* leaders' joint statement, the *Camp David Principles*, and the *Commitment to Consult* — share three load-bearing features. First, all three explicitly name the Taiwan Strait as a regional-security object: "We reaffirm the importance of peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait as an indispensable element of security and prosperity in the international community. There is no change in our basic positions on Taiwan, and we call for a peaceful resolution of cross-Strait issues." [Source 14] Second, the trilateral framework opposes coercion in regional waters in formula language directly transposable to Taiwan: "We strongly oppose any unilateral attempts to change the status quo by force or coercion." [Source 15] Third — and this is the structurally novel element — the *Commitment to Consult* obligates the three parties to "consult trilaterally with each other, in an expeditious manner, to coordinate our responses to regional challenges, provocations, and threats affecting our collective interests and security" and to "share information, align our messaging, and coordinate response actions." [Source 16] The instrument carries an explicit treaty-non-supersession clause: it "does not supersede or otherwise infringe on the commitments arising from the Treaty of Mutual Cooperation and Security Between Japan and the United States and the Mutual Defense Treaty Between the United States and the Republic of Korea. This commitment is not intended to give rise to rights or obligations under international or domestic law." [Source 16] That treaty-non-supersession clause is the load-bearing fact: the *Commitment to Consult* operates at the threshold *below* treaty-grade obligation but *above* ad-hoc consultation, and that band is precisely where Taiwan-relevant coordination has structurally lived since 1979.

The 2024 follow-on is the institutionalization step. The 15 November 2024 Joint Leaders' Statement reaffirmed the Taiwan Strait language verbatim — "We recognize that peace and stability across the Taiwan Strait is an indispensable element of security and prosperity in the Indo-Pacific region. There is no change in our basic positions on Taiwan, and we call for the peaceful resolution of cross-Strait issues." [Source 17] More structurally consequential, it announced "the establishment of the Trilateral Secretariat responsible for coordinating and implementing our shared commitments," which "will seek to ensure that the work we do together further aligns our objectives and actions to make the Indo-Pacific a thriving, connected, resilient, stable, and secure region." [Source 17] It also recorded the operationalization of the security cooperation track: "We applaud our expanding trilateral security cooperation, including the successful first two iterations of the trilateral multi-domain exercise Freedom Edge and the signing of the Memorandum of Cooperation on the Trilateral Security Cooperation Framework to institutionalize trilateral cooperation among our defense authorities." [Source 17] A standing secretariat plus an institutionalized defense-cooperation MoC plus recurring multi-domain exercises is the empirical substrate of an alliance lattice — it converts a leaders' joint statement (which is administration-bounded) into a coordination machine (which is institution-bounded).

The 2025 evidence is the survival test. There was no standalone trilateral leaders' summit in 2025 — the first calendar year of the Trump-2 administration — but the foreign-ministers' track continued. At the 22 September 2025 trilateral FM meeting, the three Ministers "reaffirmed that, in light of the increasingly severe strategic environment that the three countries face, it remains important to strengthen the solidarity among the three countries, uphold a free and open international order based on the rule of law, and continue to show the robust Japan-U.S. and U.S.-ROK alliances as well as the strategic coordination of these alliances," and "exchanged their views on regional situations, including unilateral attempts to change the status quo by force or coercion, and reaffirmed that the three countries would work closely together." [Source 18] The April 2025 (Brussels) FM-level meeting raised concerns about "strategic manoeuvres in the Taiwan Strait and East China Sea." [Source 18] That the FM track survived the leaders'-summit gap, and that the 2024-formalized Trilateral Secretariat continues to operate in 2025–2026, is direct evidence that the alliance lattice is now operating at the institution-bounded layer rather than the administration-bounded layer.

The strategic upshot for the US-Taiwan options menu is the alliance-multiplication thesis: the United States in 2026 has a coordinated US-Japan-ROK response capability for a Taiwan crisis that did not exist before 2023, anchored in two pre-existing bilateral defense treaties (US-Japan, US-ROK) plus an institutionalized trilateral consultation mechanism with explicit Taiwan Strait language. This produces a *coordination dividend* without requiring a formal US-Taiwan defense treaty: the trilateral lattice operates as the connective tissue between the two bilateral treaties, and the connective tissue is precisely what was missing in the pre-2023 architecture. From the PRC strategic-planning vantage, the relevant 2026 fact is not whether Washington has formally committed to Taiwan defense (it has not, and the legislative substrate at S1 deliberately preserves response-distribution shaping rather than declarative commitment) but whether Washington can coordinate a trilateral response with Tokyo and Seoul faster than a Taiwan crisis-window closes. The 2024 secretariat plus the *Commitment to Consult*'s "expeditious manner" language plus the Freedom Edge exercise series directly raises the probability mass on coordinated-trilateral response in the response-distribution that S1's strategic-translucency thesis describes.

This is also the connection to S3's pipeline-as-deterrent argument. The arms-sales pipeline ratifies declared US commitment in the dyad; the trilateral alliance lattice ratifies declared US commitment in the multilateral plane. The two operate together: a $32B FMS backlog *plus* a coordination-ready trilateral framework makes the response-distribution that any rational PLA planner must compute against meaningfully heavier on the high-cost-imposition tail than it was in the pre-2023 architecture. The translucent commitment is not just deeper at S1's legislative-density layer; it is also broader at S4's alliance-multiplication layer.

### S5. Economic statecraft — the durable rail

The strategic-translucency thesis from S1 — the legislative substrate persists across executives because the substrate persists — finds its most rigorous test in economic statecraft. The translation from "the executive prefers" to "the substrate sustains" is the essence of the durable-rail framing: a regulatory and statutory infrastructure constructed under one administration that subsequent administrations cannot unilaterally repeal. The CHIPS and Science Act (P.L. 117-167, enacted 2022-08-09) and the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) export-control regime — built in two interlocking interim final rules dated 2022-10-07 (87 FR 62186) and 2023-10-25 (88 FR 73424 and 88 FR 73458) — together constitute the load-bearing instance of that durable rail.

Two CHIPS Act provisions are the structural fact, not the headline appropriation. §103's "expansion clawback" places a 10-year prohibition on covered entities receiving CHIPS funds: "during the 10-year period beginning on the date of the award, subject to clause (ii), the covered entity may not engage in any significant transaction, as defined in the agreement, involving the material expansion of semiconductor manufacturing capacity in the People's Republic of China or any other foreign country of concern." [Source 19] The clawback is mirrored in the tax code by §107's recapture provision, which amends Section 50(a) of the Internal Revenue Code to provide that "if there is an applicable transaction by an applicable taxpayer before the close of the 10-year period beginning on the date such taxpayer placed in service investment credit property which is eligible for the advanced manufacturing investment credit under section 48D(a), then the tax under this chapter for the taxable year in which such transaction occurs shall be increased by 100 percent of the aggregate decrease in the credits allowed under section 38 for all prior taxable years which would have resulted solely from reducing to zero any credit determined under section 46 which is attributable to the advanced manufacturing investment credit under section 48D(a) with respect to such property." [Source 19] The 10-year horizon is the load-bearing temporal commitment: it spans at least two presidential terms and three full congressional cycles, which means the obligation matures across executive transitions by design. The translucent commitment from S1 — substrate over surface — is in CHIPS-Act §103/§107 explicitly priced into a decade-long capex incentive structure.

The "foreign country of concern" definition closes the loop, anchoring the prohibition's scope to the NDAA FY2021 §1260H statutory framework: "the term `foreign country of concern' means—(A) a country that is a covered nation (as defined in section 4872(d) of title 10 United States Code); and (B) any country that the Secretary, in consultation with the Secretary of Defense, the Secretary of State, and the Director of National Intelligence, determines to be engaged in conduct that is detrimental to the national security or foreign policy of the United States." [Source 19] This is structural translucency in tax-incentive form: the executive branch determines case-by-case scope, but the commitment to apply the prohibition is statutory, and the cross-reference to 10 U.S.C. §4872(d) hard-codes PRC inclusion at the statutory layer rather than the regulatory layer.

The BIS export-control regime is the regulatory companion. The 7 October 2022 interim final rule (87 FR 62186) introduced controls on advanced computing integrated circuits and semiconductor manufacturing equipment. The rule's SUMMARY paragraph is the canonical statement of intent: "BIS is amending the Export Administration Regulations (EAR) to implement necessary controls on advanced computing integrated circuits (ICs), computer commodities that contain such ICs, and certain semiconductor manufacturing items. In addition, BIS is expanding controls on transactions involving items for supercomputer and semiconductor manufacturing end uses, for example, this rule expands the scope of foreign-produced items subject to license requirements for twenty-eight existing entities on the Entity List that are located in China. BIS is also informing the public that specific activities of `U.S. persons' that `support' the `development' or `production' of certain ICs in the PRC require a license." [Source 20] Two operative paragraphs are load-bearing: §744.23's general prohibition on export, reexport, or in-country transfer for items destined for restricted end-uses, and §744.6(c)(2)'s extension of license requirements to U.S.-person activities that "could involve `support' for the weapons of mass destruction-related end uses." [Source 20] The U.S.-person activity provision is the structurally novel mechanism: it extends EAR jurisdiction beyond the "items subject to the EAR" framework to the activities of US-passport-holders globally, regardless of where they work or what items they handle. This is the EAR's first major extra-territorial activity-based control.

The 25 October 2023 update (88 FR 73458 and 88 FR 73424) operationalizes the 2022 framework after a year of implementation feedback. The 88 FR 73458 SUMMARY paragraph identifies the durable-rail dynamic explicitly: "Together, these IFRs revise the October 7 IFR controls to more effectively achieve BIS's focused national security policy objectives. These revisions protect U.S. national security interests by further restricting China's ability to obtain critical technologies to modernize its military capabilities in ways that threaten the national security interests of the United States and its allies." [Source 21] The 88 FR 73424 companion rule extends the 2022 controls on semiconductor manufacturing equipment with technical updates closing loops identified in public comments [Source 22]. The cadence — 87 FR 62186 (2022) → 88 FR 73424/73458 (2023) → expected FY26 update — is itself the durable-rail signature: a regulatory regime that updates annually, accumulates rather than rescinds, and is bipartisanly scaffolded by congressional NDAA-cycle entity-list authorities.

The 2026 strategic-options implication is structural. The Trump-2 administration's tariff signaling on Taiwan (a 2026-Q1 rhetorical campaign) operates at the surface layer of US-Taiwan economic relations. The durable rail — CHIPS Act 10-year clawback obligations [Source 19], BIS regulatory framework with its 2022 and 2023 IFRs and expected FY26 update cadence [Sources 20, 21, 22], and the entity-list machinery anchored in NDAA §1260H — operates underneath. The Trump-2 administration can adjust tariff rates by executive order; it cannot repeal CHIPS Act §103's 10-year clawback obligation without an act of Congress, and it cannot rescind a BIS interim final rule without the public-notice-and-comment process that produced it. ### S6. Nuclear and extended deterrence — the negative space

The translucent-commitment thesis from S1 has a counterpart in the nuclear domain — but the structural fact at the nuclear layer is *omission*, not extension. The 2022 Nuclear Posture Review (NPR), embedded in the National Defense Strategy document, is the load-bearing primary [Source 23]. Two passages, read together, fix the geometry. The first is the strategic-environment framing: "By the 2030s the United States will, for the first time in its history, face two major nuclear powers as strategic competitors and potential adversaries. This will create new stresses on stability and new challenges for deterrence, assurance, arms control, and risk reduction." [Source 23] The second is the Taiwan-specific commitment: "[The United States will] support Taiwan's asymmetric self-defense commensurate with the evolving PRC threat and consistent with our one China policy." [Source 23] What the NPR does not say is the operative fact. The same document that articulates explicit extended-deterrence commitments to NATO ("As long as nuclear weapons exist, the fundamental purpose of the U.S. nuclear arsenal is to deter nuclear attack on the United States, our allies, and partners"), to Japan, and to the Republic of Korea conspicuously withholds that formula for Taiwan. The substitution is exact and structural: where allies receive a nuclear-umbrella commitment, Taiwan receives an "asymmetric self-defense" commitment. This is not silence — silence is a TRA-grade ambiguity. This is selective articulation: a deliberately drawn boundary between conventional cost-imposition (S4 alliance scaffolding, S5 statutory rails) and nuclear extended deterrence.

The Strategic Posture Commission's 2023 final report sharpens the same omission from the threat-side [Source 24]. The Commission, a bipartisan congressionally-chartered body whose findings carry statutory weight in subsequent NDAA cycles, frames the strategic problem as a structural break: "For the first time in its history, the U.S. must deter two nuclear-armed peer adversaries (China and Russia) simultaneously. The current U.S. strategic posture is ill-prepared for this reality." [Source 24] The temporal fix is explicit: "The years 2027–2035 are identified as a high-risk period due to the rapid expansion of China's nuclear forces and Russia's continued modernization." [Source 24] The 2027–2035 window is the same Davidson-Window (S2) horizon at the nuclear layer; the Commission's report is therefore the formal congressional-record acknowledgment that the conventional-deterrence calendar (PLA-readiness 2027) and the nuclear-deterrence calendar (PRC two-peer parity 2027–2035) are now converging. PRC nuclear modernization data anchor the trajectory: the DoD 2024 China Military Power Report records that "as of mid-2024, China's operational nuclear warhead stockpile has surpassed 600", that "China is on track to possess over 1,000 operational warheads by 2030", and that "China will likely field approximately 1,500 warheads by 2035" [Source 26]. The 600 → 1,000 → 1,500 trajectory is what the Commission means by "rapid expansion" — a tripling over the high-risk decade.

The contrast with US-ROK extended deterrence is the most legible case of the asymmetric structural commitment, even though direct retrieval of the Washington Declaration's full text was egress-blocked-deferred at this fire. The April 2023 Washington Declaration established the Nuclear Consultative Group (NCG) as a bilateral institutional body whose function is to make extended deterrence operationally legible to the ROK government — a structural commitment that has no Taiwan equivalent and no analogue in any 2023–2026 US-Taiwan bilateral document [Source 25]. The asymmetry runs through every primary in this section: Taiwan receives "asymmetric self-defense" language [Source 23], statutory commitment density [S1, S5], alliance-multiplication scaffolding [S4], and arms-sales pipeline weight [S3], but does *not* receive the institutionalized extended-deterrence consultation track that the ROK and Japan receive. The negative-space framing is therefore precise: the US has structurally hardened every Taiwan-relevant commitment that operates *below* the nuclear threshold, and has structurally withheld every commitment that operates *at* the nuclear threshold.

This produces a specific 2026 strategic-options reading. The PLA planner facing an invasion-decision matrix in 2027–2030 must compute against legislative-substrate commitment (S1), Davidson-Window readiness signaling (S2), arms-sales pipeline backlog (S3), trilateral alliance scaffolding (S4), durable-rail economic statecraft (S5) — and against the absence of an explicit US nuclear-umbrella commitment to Taiwan. The first five layers are credible, statute-anchored, and increasing; the sixth layer is non-existent at the doctrinal level but contingent at the geographic-adjacency level (the US-Japan and US-ROK extended-deterrence postures are second-order Taiwan deterrents because the operational geography of any cross-strait conflict overlaps the operational geography of the US-Japan alliance). The resulting structural inference, which the GWW3 frame at S8 will need to encode, is that the 2026 PRC strategic-decision problem is not "will the US use nuclear weapons over Taiwan" — that question has a structural answer ("not as a primary commitment") encoded in NPR-2022 by omission — but rather "will the geographic proximity of US treaty-ally extended-deterrence commitments make a Taiwan operation strategically inseparable from a war with Japan or the ROK". The 2027–2035 high-risk window identified by the Strategic Posture Commission is precisely the window in which the second-order-deterrence answer becomes structurally underdetermined: as PRC warhead count moves through 600 → 1,000 → 1,500, the US extended-deterrence posture toward Japan and ROK must adapt to two-peer reality, and Taiwan-second-order-coverage becomes a residual of those adaptations rather than a dyadic commitment. The Commission's framing — "ill-prepared for this reality" — is the structural admission that this residual is currently being computed without the analytical posture to compute it well.
