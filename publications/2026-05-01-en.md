## Executive summary

The Silicon Shield is a doctrine, not a fact, and like every doctrine it depends on assumptions that can be examined and broken. Today's analysis isolates three coupled assumptions inside the doctrine — mutual vulnerability, rational-actor weighting of economic cost, and irreversibility of disruption — and shows that each one is being eroded on a different clock by a different player, with the leading-edge node staying on Taiwan through at least 2030 acting as the hard floor under the entire structure. The GWW3 modeling task that falls out of this is to make Pottinger's 2022 rejection of the rational-actor assumption an explicit player parameter rather than burying it inside an assumed equilibrium — which is exactly the kind of move the Belief Subgraph was built to support.

## Today's thesis

**The chip supply chain is a single-point-of-failure dressed as a deterrent.** Roughly seventy percent of global foundry-market revenue and over ninety percent of leading-edge production sit on a strip of western Taiwan within about 250 km of the strait [Source 1, Source 2]. The Silicon Shield argument says that very concentration deters conflict because disruption would hurt all major parties simultaneously. But the argument silently bundles three claims, and only one of them — that the leading-edge node stays on Taiwan through 2030 — is currently robust. The other two — that the major parties weight expected economic cost rationally, and that the overseas footprint cannot quickly substitute — are each under active erosion. A model that treats the Silicon Shield as a binary deterrent will be wrong about the next four years; a model that treats it as a time-dependent parameter with three independently moving subcomponents has a chance.

## Research findings

### Thread 1 — TSMC's dominance and the geography of the fab footprint

TSMC holds approximately seventy percent of global foundry-market revenue and produces over ninety percent of the world's most-advanced-generation chips [Source 1, Source 2]. The "advanced generation" — currently 5 nm and below, with N2 entering production at Hsinchu Fab 22 — is the terminal node for the AI-accelerator and high-end smartphone supply chains. Samsung Foundry holds second place at 5 nm and 3 nm but with different IP libraries and persistently weaker yield; Intel Foundry Services is years behind in customer adoption. Substitution at the leading edge is a multi-year capital and learning-curve problem, not a procurement problem.

The geography is the single most consequential fact in the doctrine. TSMC operates fabs at Hsinchu (Fabs 2, 3, 5, 8, 12A, 12B, 20), Taichung (Fab 15), Tainan (Fab 18), Shanhua (Fabs 6, 14), and Kaohsiung (Fab 22) — virtually the entire fab footprint sits on the western corridor of Taiwan within about 250 km of the strait [Source 1]. 2025 production was 15.02 million 12-inch equivalent wafers [Source 1]. The diversification effort is real but trails:

- **Arizona Fab 21** — Phase 1 operational (N4/N5 trailing nodes); Phase 2 mass production targeted for 2028; Phases 3-5 targeted for 2030 [Source 1].
- **Kumamoto Japan Fab 23** — Commercial operations began December 2024; second adjacent fab under construction as of January 2025 [Source 1].
- **Dresden Germany** — Planned operational in 2029, monthly capacity 40,000 12-inch wafers [Source 1].

The diversification timeline is the load-bearing point: even fully built out, the overseas footprint is *trailing-node* (Arizona Phase 1 = N4/N5, not the leading edge), and the leading-edge node stays on Taiwan through at least 2030. The Silicon Shield doctrine bets on this concentration. Diversification dilutes it on the trailing edge first.

### Thread 2 — ASML's monopoly and the export-control regime

Upstream of TSMC sits a second concentration: ASML holds approximately eighty-three percent of worldwide lithography-machine sales and a near-monopoly in EUV [Source 3]. The High-NA platform (numerical aperture 0.55, roughly $370 M per system) shipped first to Intel in December 2023 and then to TSMC in late 2024 — those are the only confirmed High-NA recipients to date [Source 3]. EUV is the necessary enabling tooling for sub-7 nm at production-scale economics; without it, advanced-node production requires multi-patterning DUV with severe cost and yield penalties (see Thread 3).

The Dutch export-control regime against Chinese EUV and DUV access has tightened in five formal steps over seven years [Source 3]:

- **2018** — first Trump-era US pressure on ASML to block EUV sales to China.
- **March 2023** — Dutch government places restrictions on chip-equipment exports.
- **September 2023** — export-license requirements come into effect.
- **January 2024** — restrictions extended to additional advanced equipment.
- **September 6, 2024** — tighter controls on certain ASML chipmaking equipment.
- **January 2025** — restrictions expanded; ASML now applies to the Dutch government directly rather than via the US channel — a regime-formalization step that matters because it removes the diplomatic out for Beijing of negotiating with Washington above the Hague.

The five-step ratchet is not a US-imposed policy on the Netherlands; it is a Dutch-internalized regime that now operates with US coordination but Dutch ownership. That distinction will matter for any GWW3 model that needs to ask which player can soften the constraint.

### Thread 3 — SMIC's DUV workaround and its structural ceiling

SMIC operates the **N+3 process** as of December 2025, confirmed by the TechInsights teardown of Huawei's Kirin 9030 (a November 2025 product) [Source 4]. The process is a "scaled evolution of 7 nm-class" using **DUV immersion lithography with self-aligned quadruple patterning (SAQP)** — explicitly *not* EUV [Source 4]. SMIC has been on the US Entity List since December 2020 [Source 4]. The trajectory:

- 7 nm officially established July 2022.
- 5 nm-equivalent capability deployed by late 2024 (Kirin 9000s September 2023, Kirin 9030 November 2025).

Wikipedia is silent on yield rates; industry reports — uncited here, to verify with TechInsights or SemiAnalysis primaries on Day 4+ — suggest roughly 50% yield versus TSMC's 90%-plus at equivalent nodes, driving 2-3× higher per-die cost. The DUV-with-SAQP route works for boutique-volume strategic products (Huawei flagship phones, datacenter AI accelerators in tens of thousands of units), but it does not scale to civilian economic dependence: pricing dynamics break before volume does. This is the structural ceiling.

The ceiling has two components, and they are independent: (1) a *cost* ceiling because multi-patterning multiplies mask layers and yield losses, and (2) a *node* ceiling because beyond a certain density-per-area, diffraction-limited DUV cannot achieve the required pitch even with quadruple patterning. The second ceiling is the harder of the two; it cannot be solved with money. EUV access is the only known path past it, and the export-control regime is what gates EUV access.

## Game-theoretic framing (GWW3)

### Framing 1 — Asymmetric incomplete information on the substitution timeline

PRC, US, and Taiwan players in the chip game face structurally **different information sets** on when SMIC closes the gap to leading-edge TSMC. The PRC information set treats N+3 deployment as proof-of-concept that EUV can be substituted; the US/Taiwan information set treats the same deployment as boutique-only, with the structural cost ceiling intact. **Both can be coherent under their own priors.** Neither is currently testing the other's hypothesis under common-knowledge conditions.

This is a **signaling-game** structure, not a complete-information bargaining game. The Belief Subgraph model from GWW3 will need to carry separate belief states for the PRC state, PRC firms, and PRC citizenry — they diverge sharply on substitution feasibility, and the divergence matters because PRC-state policy is constrained by which beliefs it can credibly export to its own constituencies. A model that collapses the three beliefs into one "PRC view" loses the dynamic that actually drives the timeline.

### Framing 2 — The Silicon Shield as a Pottinger assumption-set

The Silicon Shield doctrine [Source 2] requires three coupled assumptions to function as a deterrent:

- **A1 (mutual vulnerability):** disruption hurts all major parties simultaneously, in roughly comparable magnitudes after factoring in time-to-substitute.
- **A2 (rational-actor):** all major parties weight expected economic cost in their conflict-decision calculus, with discount rates and risk preferences in a comparable family.
- **A3 (irreversibility):** chip-supply disruption from a kinetic conflict cannot be quickly mitigated by overseas capacity, holding the deterrent threat credibly above the cost of restraint.

Matt Pottinger's 2022 critique [Source 2] specifically rejects A2 for PRC decision-making. He argues that PRC strategic culture treats certain political objectives — Taiwan reunification on PRC terms among them — as effectively non-fungible against economic cost. **The GWW3 model needs to make this rejection-or-acceptance an explicit player parameter**, not bury it inside an assumed equilibrium. If A2 is even partially relaxed for the PRC player, the deterrent effect of the Silicon Shield drops nonlinearly — and the model should expose the shape of that nonlinearity rather than hide it inside the rational-equilibrium computation.

The technical move here is to add a parameter `θ_econ_weight ∈ [0, 1]` per player, where 1 = full economic-cost weighting and 0 = political-objective dominance, and to compute the deterrent effect as a function of `θ_econ_weight × A1 × A3`. The literature debate over Pottinger's claim then becomes a debate over the value of `θ_econ_weight` for the PRC player — a structurally cleaner argument than "is China rational."

### Framing 3 — The decoupling timeline as a Schelling clock

If A1 erodes — that is, if overseas capacity reaches threshold X% of global advanced-node production by year Y — the Silicon Shield assumption-set fails by construction, because disruption would no longer hurt all major parties symmetrically. The CHIPS Act ($50 B subsidies, Biden 2022) and the Trump-administration pivot to American manufacturing [Source 2] are both *accelerators of A1-erosion*. The diversification timeline (Thread 1) is therefore not just industrial policy; it is an active depletion of the doctrine that protects the asset.

The model should produce a **time-dependent deterrence parameter** that decays with the global-share-of-Taiwan-fabs metric. Calibration target: per the public TSMC roadmap, leading-edge stays on Taiwan through 2030; the deterrence parameter has a hard floor through that horizon. After 2030, the floor lifts — Arizona Phase 3-5 and Dresden are projected operational, and the trailing-node concentration on Taiwan begins to ease. The deterrence half-life is the right summary statistic; the GWW3 simulator should report it as a first-class output rather than recompute it per scenario.

### Framing 4 — Path-dependence, again

Day 2's recognition-diplomacy thesis ("the Markov property fails") has a chip-supply analogue: **substitution capacity is path-dependent on tooling, talent, intellectual property, and regulatory chronology — not just on capex.** SMIC's N+3 timing depends on when ASML last shipped DUV scanners pre-September-2023 controls and on SMIC's hoarding of spares; that is a path-dependence on *regulatory chronology*, not a stationary parameter. The same modeling discipline applies as on Day 2: do not model substitution as a memoryless process even when the textbook formulation is tempting. The state of the substitution game in 2027 will depend on which DUV tools were in which Chinese fab on which date in 2023, and that fact will not be derivable from a steady-state model.

## Hassaleh-Nexus implications

The financial-market signals that would move under each scenario are unusually well-localized for a geopolitical analysis, because the chip supply chain compresses an enormous amount of macro risk into a small number of public tickers. The Nexus model should track at minimum:

- **TSMC ADR (TSM) and Taiwan-listed (2330.TW)** — the direct measure. Implied volatility surfaces for these names are the first-order signal of market-implied probability of cross-strait disruption.
- **ASML (ASML.AS, ASML)** — the upstream chokepoint. Equity-price reaction to each formal step of the Dutch export-control regime ratchet (Thread 2) is the cleanest available natural experiment for measuring market-implied price elasticity of regulatory chronology.
- **SOXX and SMH ETFs** — the diversified-exposure measures. Spread between the two ETFs and TSM directly is a cleaner measure of *concentration risk* than either alone.
- **NVIDIA (NVDA)** — the demand-side single name most exposed to TSMC leading-edge production. Implied-vol skew on NVDA options is the demand-side proxy for the same disruption-risk surface that TSM implied-vol measures from the supply side.
- **Yen and Korean won cross-rates against USD** — secondary channels via Kumamoto fab exposure (Japan) and Samsung Foundry as the primary substitute (Korea). The cross-rates carry the regional-macro channel that single-name equities cannot.

The Nexus modeling task that falls out: build a graph node for each of these instruments, edge them to the Silicon Shield assumption-set parameters (A1, A2, A3 plus `θ_econ_weight`), and let the simulator query "if A2 weakens by Δ, how does the implied-vol surface on TSM 2330 6-month ATM move?" The historical natural experiments (Pelosi August 2022 visit, the 2023-2025 export-control ratchet steps) are the calibration set. This is a clean piece of work for Inanna's structural review pass and Nisaba's audit; both should be involved before the model goes live.
